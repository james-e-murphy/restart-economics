# Pre-analysis plan

*Restart Economics for AI Agents: Cutoffs, Retries, and the Cost of a Resolved Task*

Written before the data audit and before any policy result was computed. It is frozen, with the audit's findings in Section 10, at the `plan-frozen` tag, and every policy result is computed after that tag. After it, departures are recorded as deviations in AUDIT.md rather than as amendments here.

---

## 1. Question

An agent execution on a task consumes a random amount of money and ends in success or failure. Repeated attempts at the same task under the same configuration differ enormously in cost: Bai et al. report runs on one task differing by up to thirty times in total tokens. An operator therefore does not run an attempt; they run a policy: cap the attempt, retry, escalate to a person. Average cost per task prices none of this, because it assumes every attempt runs to termination and averages over a distribution whose dispersion is the whole opportunity.

What motivates a cap is not that failures cost more than successes. Pooled across tasks they do, but that comparison confounds outcome with difficulty, since hard tasks both cost more and fail more; computed within task on the subset of tasks that produced both outcomes, earlier work on one of these configurations found the difference near zero, with an interval spanning zero in both directions. Two things motivate a cap instead, and both are measured here rather than assumed: dispersion within task, which is what a restart exploits, and tail composition, the share of spend beyond a threshold that ends in resolution, which is what a cutoff decision actually turns on. The near-zero within-task premium is also a prior for question 4: if cost alone does not separate failures from successes within a task, a state rule conditioning on spend has less to work with than it might appear.

This paper asks what a resolved task costs under the policies operators actually run, and which parts of such a policy earn their keep. In order:

1. Does retrying a failed attempt pay?
2. Does capping the attempts improve the retries?
3. Does varying the cap by attempt improve it further?
4. Does observing an execution's state beat a fixed schedule?
5. Does switching models between attempts improve it again?

Each is a step on one ladder, scored on one number, so that every step's credit is incremental to the step before it.

## 2. Framework

A Las Vegas algorithm is a randomized procedure whose running time is random and whose output, whenever it terminates, is correct. An agent is not quite that, because it can terminate normally and be wrong. What makes the framework usable is a verifier, and it is exact only with respect to the verifier's acceptance event: an agent plus a deterministic acceptance test is Las-Vegas-like for the event "the test accepts."

For such procedures the economics of stopping is settled. Luby, Sinclair and Zuckerman (1993) consider strategies that are sequences of cutoffs, with each attempt independent, and show that when the runtime distribution is known the strategy minimizing expected time to success is a constant sequence: run to a cutoff, and if unsuccessful, restart. This paper borrows that setup and that observation, not the theorems of that paper, which concern the universal strategy for unknown distributions and are not used here. Two extensions are stated rather than assumed. The classical objective is time, whereas here it is cost; the renewal argument survives any cost nondecreasing in iterations. And the classical procedure has no restart overhead, whereas every agent attempt pays for environment setup and initial context before it does anything, which is what makes very short cutoffs lose.

The literature that grew from that result maps onto the ladder. A constant cutoff is the classical static policy. Attempt-indexed schedules are the ensemble case: across heterogeneous tasks of unknown type a constant cutoff is no longer optimal among sequences, because a short first attempt can catch easy tasks and a longer later attempt can be given to hard ones, with no state observation at all. State-aware stopping is the dynamic-restart problem. Switching models between attempts is the algorithm-portfolio problem, where combining procedures pays when their failures on the same instance are weakly correlated.

The paper is also a sequential generalization of Cost-of-Pass (Erol et al., ICLR 2026), whose expected cost of one attempt divided by success probability is the limit of the estimand below, and whose frontier takes the minimum over models and a human expert, which is where the outside option comes from. That work found that performance-oriented inference-time techniques rarely reduce cost-of-pass while budget-aware ones show promise. A capped restart is the budget-aware sequential analogue. Section 7 states in advance what the audit's magnitudes lead one to expect of it.

## 3. Estimand

**Policy value is expected cost per incoming task.** For task *i*, cutoff *t*, per-attempt probability *p_i(t)* of benchmark resolution within the cutoff, expected truncated attempt cost *c_i(t)*, probability *r_i(t)* that an attempt terminates with a candidate to verify, verification cost *v* per candidate, at most *K* fresh attempts, and an outside option that resolves the task for certain at cost *H_i*:

    C_i(t, K, H, v) = [c_i(t) + v · r_i(t)] · Σ_{j=0}^{K−1} [1 − p_i(t)]^j + H_i · [1 − p_i(t)]^K

The terms are agent spend, verification of what the agent produced, the expected number of attempts made, and the outside option paid when every attempt fails. Because the outside option resolves the task, every task resolves: resolution rate is one by construction, and policy value is also cost per resolved task. Reported beside it is the share of tasks resolved without invoking the outside option, which is the agent's own contribution.

Three properties are deliberate.

**The estimand is finite and requires no extrapolation.** The infinite-restart limit, cost per attempt divided by success probability, has no finite value for a task the configuration cannot solve, and four attempts cannot identify a per-task success probability: zero successes in four leaves a one-sided 95 percent bound near 53 percent. The finite form avoids both.

**Abandonment is never free.** Without a priced outside option a policy could look cheap by giving up on hard tasks, which would reintroduce a cost-versus-success tradeoff into what is meant to be a single number.

**The outside option is an external, task-specific scale.** *H_i* is a shadow price, the cost of guaranteed outside resolution, not a claim that a human would in fact fix the task. SWE-bench Verified carries a per-task annotation of the time an engineer with a few hours of familiarity with the codebase would need, in four buckets: under 15 minutes, 15 minutes to an hour, 1 to 4 hours, and over 4 hours. *H_i* is that time at a stated fully loaded engineer rate, with the buckets converted to point estimates by their geometric means as in METR's Table 8: 3.9, 30.0, 120.0 and 480.0 minutes. The rate is the swept parameter, so every break-even is expressed in wage rates rather than dollars per task.

The annotations run optimistic, and not uniformly. METR baselined six tasks and found the four drawn from the under-15-minute bucket took 8, 26, 67 and 84 minutes, a geometric mean of 32.9 against the bucket's 3.9, while two tasks from the 1-to-4-hour bucket averaged 131.6 against 120.0. The understatement is therefore concentrated in the short bucket, by roughly a factor of eight there and by about a tenth in the long one, on a sample of six tasks and seven baselines. The prespecified sensitivity is accordingly bucket-specific rather than a single multiplier, using METR's measured baseline means where they exist and interpolating for the two buckets they did not baseline, with the small sample behind it stated. Because SWE-bench Verified is weighted toward short tasks, this sensitivity moves *H_i* for most of the population and is not a minor robustness check.

**The success event is benchmark resolution**, acceptance by the benchmark's evaluation tests. That is not target resolution, a mergeable and correct fix. METR's March 2026 note had four maintainers from three SWE-bench Verified repositories review 296 AI-generated pull requests that the automated grader scored as correct, and found that roughly half would not be merged into main after adjusting for noise against 47 human-written golden patches, with maintainer merge decisions about 24 percentage points below the automated scores. An independent estimate on a different benchmark, SWE-Gate, found 221 of 644 test-passing agent patches violated constraints drawn from the original pull-request reviews. The two standards are distinguished throughout, and one prespecified sensitivity treats a fraction φ of test-passing attempts as unresolved and charges the outside option later, with φ set at freeze from these sources and reported at both ends of the range they imply, roughly a third to a half.

Three limits on that sensitivity are recorded rather than hidden. METR's study covers three of the twelve repositories and 95 of the 500 issues, so its rate is not measured on the full task population. Its most recent agent is Claude Sonnet 4.5, so it does not cover the more recent configurations in this data. And its unit is a pull request reviewed by a maintainer, not an attempt terminated by a policy. A single φ assumes the false-accept fraction is the same under every policy, which may fail if patches produced after a cutoff-driven retry are more superficial than those from uninterrupted runs. The assumption is stated, not tested.

## 4. Data

Four OpenHands runs on all 500 SWE-bench Verified tasks per configuration, from the release accompanying Bai et al., *How Do AI Agents Spend Your Money?* (arXiv 2604.22750): `loong0814/openhands_trajectories`, nine archives, of which the audit admits seven configurations and 13,999 executions (Section 10).

These are **configurations**, not models: a run is a model under a harness version at a date through an endpoint under a price schedule, and every claim is about the configuration. AUDIT.md establishes what each one was, before this plan is frozen, and its findings are recorded in Section 10.

**Cost is reconstructed from physical quantities.** The derived data layer holds tokens by class, calls, iterations and elapsed time; pricing is a separate pure function applied under a dated schedule. A policy whose cutoff is set in dollars is a different policy under a different schedule, so a price sensitivity refits the cutoff rather than rescaling a table.

**The primary cutoff is denominated in agent iterations**, the harness's own unit and the knob an operator sets. Iterations and LLM calls could differ, because history condensation adds calls and retries may add more, so the audit verified the iteration-to-call mapping per configuration. It found one call per iteration and no condensation anywhere, so the grid is stated in calls (Section 10). A dollar-denominated cutoff under one stated schedule is a prespecified sensitivity family, fitted separately; the optimizer may not choose between the two families after the fact.

**Transfer features are provider-agnostic.** Cross-configuration transfer uses call count, cumulative output tokens, and the recent rate of output tokens. Elapsed time was registered as a feature and is dropped, because Qwen logs no call times; the condensation indicator is moot, because no configuration condenses history. Input tokens per step are excluded, because cache classes have no common semantics across providers. The within-configuration state rule uses the same state, so that within and across configurations differ only in where the models are fitted (Section 6).

**The baseline is the harness's own stopping** — the agent's finish, its stuck-in-loop detector, or the iteration limit — not "run to completion." The loop detector is a crude state-aware stopping rule already inside the incumbent, so question 4 measures the value of execution state beyond it.

## 5. Identification and estimation

**Identification is by design.** Every policy in the class stops at or before the point where the harness stopped, so the outcome under any such policy is a deterministic function of the logged trajectory: a prefix, truncated. Policy value is estimable by replay, with no ignorability assumption, no propensity model and no importance weights. Harness terminations are therefore not censoring: continuation past a loop-detector or iteration-limit stop is outside the policy class, not unidentified within it. What is not estimable is the value of removing the harness's own rules, and no such claim is made.

**Estimation is by enumeration, not by plugging in probabilities.** For *K* ≤ 4 the four draws are enumerated exactly: 24 orderings per task for one configuration, 16 attempt pairs for a two-configuration cascade. The enumeration requires a task's runs to be exchangeable, any ordering as likely as any other, not independent: a configuration that sometimes repeats a trajectory is priced correctly by enumeration, the repetition included, which is what a retry of that configuration would meet. The cascade estimator additionally requires independence between configurations, which separate runs provide. A task observed at 0/4 contributes *K* failed attempts plus the outside option; no resolution probability is imputed, and nothing is claimed about that task's true conditional expected cost. The claim is the population one: with a policy fixed out of sample, averaging the permutation estimator over tasks is unbiased for the finite-*K* policy value.

**A cut-off attempt is discarded unverified.** The harness instead submits whatever patch is in the workspace at its iteration limit, and some of those resolve, but the logged data identify only discard, because the workspace at an earlier iteration is not recoverable without re-execution. Stop-and-submit can only raise the chance of resolution while incurring verification cost on every partial patch, so the two are not ordered a priori on total cost: with an automated verifier the submit variant weakly dominates and the measured cutoff value is conservative; with a human verifier and a low-yield partial patch, discard can be cheaper. The identified object and the direction of the difference in each regime are stated; no bound is claimed.

**Selection is cross-fitted and uncertainty is propagated.** One outer split of the 500 tasks into five task-grouped folds. Every quantity chosen from data — the cutoff, the attempt budget, the schedule, the state rule, the cascade — is chosen on training folds and scored once on held-out folds. That removes evaluation optimism but does not by itself carry selection variation into the intervals, so intervals come from a full-pipeline bootstrap: resample tasks, redo the training-fold selection and fitting inside each replicate, score again on that replicate's held-out tasks. Where refitting inside every replicate is too expensive, the replicate count is reduced and the intervals are stated as conditional on the fitted selection procedure rather than claimed to include it. Intervals are 95 percent percentile intervals from 1,000 replicates, or from the reduced count, which is then reported. No significance test is run; a margin is described as positive or negative only when its interval excludes zero. The inferential population is a workload drawn like SWE-bench Verified, so the task-cluster bootstrap is primary; a two-stage bootstrap that also resamples attempts within task is the sensitivity reflecting the four-draw horizon.

**Transfer holds out both the configuration and the tasks.** For target configuration *m* and outer fold *f*, fit on the other six configurations with fold *f*'s tasks excluded, and score on *m*'s tasks in *f*. Fitting on the same tasks through other configurations would be a different and easier test. The licensed claim is configuration-level generality within SWE-bench Verified under this harness, with each configuration held out in turn; it is not domain generality.

**The verifier's accuracy is the benchmark's; its cost is a parameter.** Every result is reported at two prespecified regimes: an automated verifier with *v* near zero, and a human verifier with *v* at 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5 of the outside option, so that the reader can see which policies survive the move from tests to review. Under the human regime a cap earns a second credit, since a cut-off attempt saves a review as well as tokens.

**The information set is the same for every policy.** In the primary ladder no policy observes the task's own outside-option cost before or during execution; *H_i* enters only when welfare is scored, and the state rule's continue-or-restart comparison uses the population mean outside option at the stated rate. Ex-ante task information is admitted only in the heterogeneity display, and there it is admitted to every policy at once.

## 6. Policies

All policies are attempt-indexed schedules over (configuration, cutoff) pairs, evaluated on the same terms with the same outside option and the same verifier.

| Step | Policy | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| i | One attempt, no cutoff, then the outside option | Baseline |
| ii | *K* attempts, no cutoff, then the outside option | The value of retrying at all |
| iii | *K* attempts at a constant cutoff | The value of a cap |
| iii-b | *K* attempts under a schedule (*t₁*, …, *t_K*) | The value of varying the cap by attempt, without state |
| iv | State-aware stopping | The value of observing the execution |
| v | Schedule over ((*m₁*, *t₁*), …, (*m_K*, *t_K*)) | The value of switching configurations |

Steps iii-b and v are one family: iii-b is the case where every attempt uses the same configuration, and v lets it change. After a failed attempt an operator chooses what to try next, and trying the same configuration again is one of the choices, so retry and reroute are one decision.

**The state-aware policy.** The full finite-horizon problem defines the value of continuing by backward induction over the decision grid, with resolution and failure before the next decision point as terminal events. The grid is at most thirty-five decision points by four attempts, so the problem is small; learning the state transition it requires is not. The policy evaluated here is therefore a **receding-horizon restart rule**, named as such and not as the value function: at each decision point it compares the expected cost of committing this attempt to termination — fitted remaining spend, plus the value of restarting weighted by the fitted probability of not resolving — against the value of restarting now, then re-evaluates at the next point. Because the commit-to-termination cost ignores the option to stop later, it overstates the cost of continuing, which biases the rule toward restarting. Its restart values, specified below, can pull either way, so the net direction is measured on synthetic data rather than asserted (Section 8). The population-optimal dynamic program over the same state and action space weakly dominates it by construction; this study does not estimate that additional margin.

**Decision opportunities are common to every policy**: every fifth call from 5 to 100, then every twenty-fifth from 125 to 475, below each configuration's own cap. That is 19 decision points for GPT-5, whose cap is 100, and 35 for the six capped at 500, where between 2 and 21 percent of resolved attempts run past 100 calls. A constant cutoff stops at a fixed decision point regardless of state. The state rule, the threshold comparator and the dollar cutoffs act at the same points; a dollar cutoff stops an attempt at the first decision point at which its spend exceeds the cutoff, over 40 cutoffs spaced evenly in logarithm between the 5th percentile of spend at call 5 and the largest attempt spend on the training folds. Extra opportunities may not masquerade as the value of richer state. The common-horizon sensitivity uses the points up to 95 and stops any attempt still running at 100.

**The policy class is stated and given a low-complexity comparator.** Because replay is exact, the fitted regressions do no work in evaluation; they only choose the policy, and a policy chosen on 500 tasks carries selection regret scaling with the complexity of its class. The class is therefore fixed here.

- **State.** The state of a running attempt at decision point *t* is three variables: log *t*; the log of one plus its cumulative output tokens; and the log of one plus its output tokens over the last five calls.
- **Models.** Two models are fitted on every usable attempt still running at a decision point, pooled across decision points and attempts:
  - a logistic regression for the probability *q* that the attempt resolves if run to its end;
  - a log-link quasi-Poisson regression for its remaining spend. Remaining spend is measured in multiples of the attempt's own spend per call over the last five calls, so the target has no currency, and the prediction *s* is converted to dollars at that rate.
  
  That is eight coefficients per configuration, none specific to a decision point.
- **Decision.** At decision point *t* of attempt *j*, the rule stops the attempt, discarding it unverified, when *s* + *v* > *q* · *V*_{j+1}.
  - *V*_{j+1} is the value of starting afresh with the attempts that remain. *V*_{K+1} is the population mean outside option at the rate being evaluated, and *v* is the verification cost at that mean.
  - The rule treats an attempt run to its end as producing a candidate to verify. Empty patches are under 1.5 percent of attempts in every configuration, and replay charges *v* only on attempts that do produce one.
  - Under the φ sensitivity, a resolution is worth φ·*H̄* less, and the inequality becomes *s* + *v* > *q* · (*V*_{j+1} − φ·*H̄*).
- **Restart values and *K*.** For each *K* from 1 to 4, the restart values are computed on the training folds backward over attempts: *V*_{j} is the replayed mean cost of attempts *j* to *K* under the rule, given *V*_{j+1}. *K* is chosen as for every policy: the value with the lowest replayed *V*_1 on training-fold tasks with four usable draws. Everything that depends on the rate is recomputed at each rate in the sweep: the restart values, *K*, and the rule's decisions. The two models do not depend on the rate and are fitted once per fold.
- **What the restart values do not capture.** They are averages over tasks, not conditional on the task at hand, so they understate the cost of restarting on a task that is going badly. Because the rule replaying itself is not optimal, they also overstate the cost of restarting relative to the optimal policy. The two effects run in opposite directions.
- **Within and across configurations.** The same state and form serve both, so the two differ only in where the two models are fitted: on the configuration's own training folds, or on the other six configurations' (Section 5). For transfer, the six configurations' rows are pooled with weights that give each configuration equal total weight, so that configurations with long attempts do not dominate. In both cases the restart values and *K* come from the target configuration's own training folds. Transfer therefore tests whether reading an execution's state carries across configurations, not whether a configuration's success rate does.
- **What is not in the state.** Elapsed time is not a state variable, because Qwen logs no call times. No configuration condenses history, so there is no condensation indicator.

Beside the plug-in rule sits one comparator: empirical welfare maximization over a two-parameter threshold family. It stops at decision point *t* if spend rate exceeds *a* + *b*·*t*, where spend rate is dollars per call over the last five calls. It is chosen by grid search to maximize replayed value on training folds, with *K* chosen alongside *a* and *b* as for every policy, and is scored on held-out folds, within configuration only. The grid takes:
- *a* at the 5th to 95th percentiles, in steps of five, of spend rate over running attempts at decision points in the training folds;
- *b* at eleven values that move the threshold at the last decision point from a quarter of *a* to four times *a*, evenly in logarithm.

It is a deliberately low-complexity class whose out-of-sample performance regularizes the comparison; no numerical regret bound is claimed. If the plug-in rule cannot beat it, the regressions are not adding information.

## 7. Prespecified comparisons and exhibits

**Primary result.** The cap's marginal value given retry — step iii minus step ii, expected cost per incoming task — as a function of the outside-option rate, in both verifier regimes, per configuration and as the median across configurations. Its summary statistic is the break-even rate at which the cap's marginal value changes sign, one per configuration per regime, expressed in dollars per hour and in multiples of the configuration's median attempt cost. Uniqueness is not assumed: under automated verification a higher rate makes each lost success more expensive, but under human verification the review a discarded attempt saves also scales with the rate, and policies are re-selected at every rate. If the sign changes more than once over the sweep, every crossing is reported. If it never changes, the break-even lies outside the sweep and the side is stated.

The prespecified expectation, stated from magnitudes established in the audit and not from any policy result: the annotated outside option exceeds observed attempt cost by two orders of magnitude for the first configuration audited, so under automated verification the cap is not expected to pay at any rate an enterprise would recognize, because the escalation cost of each success it cuts off exceeds the agent spend it saves; under human verification it is expected to pay, because a discarded attempt saves a review as well as tokens. Two regimes, opposite answers, from one policy class, is the result the design is built to detect.

**Reported beside it, not as the headline.** The value of retry, step ii against step i, which the same magnitudes predict to be large at any human-scale rate; and the full ladder, so that the cap's marginal value can be read against retry's.

**Primary transfer:** the state rule's cross-configuration margin over the best schedule, in the automated-verifier regime. The best schedule is step iii-b's, which contains the constant cutoff, and like the rule's restart values it is fitted on the target configuration's own training folds. The margin is reported per target configuration and as the median across the seven, as a function of the rate on both axes of the sweep, as the primary result is. The abstract and the first figure are built from the primary result and the primary transfer; everything else is secondary and reported in full.

**The outside-option sweep** runs on two axes: from $5 to $300 per hour, and from 0.5 to 500 times each configuration's median attempt cost, so that break-evens are comparable across configurations whose attempts differ manyfold in cost. The low end of the sweep is deliberately far below any human rate; that is the regime of a cheap automated fallback, a deferral, or a dropped task, and it is where the cap's value becomes visible.

**Main-text exhibits.** A table of policy value by configuration for steps i through iii-b, across the rate sweep and both verifier regimes, with break-even rates and the breakpoints where the optimal *K* and cutoff change. A figure of the state rule's margin over the best schedule, within and across configurations. A figure of the best cross-configuration schedule against each single configuration's optimum.

**Diagnostics, presented beside the result they explain.** The share of log-spend variance that is within task, and the share of tasks with mixed outcomes across four runs, per configuration: both motivate restarts and neither bounds their value, since restart value depends jointly on the cost and success distributions, their dependence, the outside option, setup cost, *K* and the cutoff. Tail composition: for each cutoff on the grid, the share of spend incurred beyond it by executions that ultimately resolve, with a companion curve for the probability of resolution given survival to that point, and the cutoff marked at the annotated *H_i*. Cross-configuration outcome correlation, seven by seven, the algorithm-portfolio predictor of whether a cascade pays.

**Appendix, reported in full and not argued from.** The dollar cutoff family. The universal restart schedule as a comparator, whose classical guarantee assumes unbounded restarts of a procedure that eventually succeeds and so is not claimed to carry over. The threshold comparator's details. Leave-one-out essentialness of each configuration to the best cascade. A sample oracle benchmark: for each task the cheapest policy in the same schedule class the cascades are drawn from, evaluated on that task's own draws and averaged, which bounds the evaluated class but carries winner's-curse bias downward from taking a per-task minimum over seven configurations on four draws, and is therefore a benchmark rather than a population ceiling; the gap between it and the best cross-fitted cascade is an upper estimate of the value of knowing the task in advance. The state rule's margin when restricted to acting only at the first decision point, which if large would recommend one early look and a fixed rule thereafter. A heterogeneity display of the cap's value by difficulty bucket, where ex-ante task information is admitted to every policy at once; 500 tasks do not support finer covariates. The 95th percentile of cost per task under each policy, because a cap is partly insurance and mean cost alone undervalues it. The spread in policy value across configurations against the spread across policies within a configuration, which asks whether choosing the configuration or the policy moves cost more. A median-cost version of the primary comparison.

## 8. Sequence and verification

The audit of AUDIT.md precedes plan freeze. Then extraction; the ladder through step iii-b; the state rule within configuration; transfer; cascades; the heterogeneity display.

**The evaluator is verified before it is trusted.** A synthetic dry run with known answers exercises the whole pipeline first: it checks that the enumerator recovers a known optimal cutoff and *K*, that the full-pipeline bootstrap covers at the nominal rate for a ratio estimand under heavy-tailed spend, that cross-fitting removes in-sample optimism, and, where the exact dynamic program is computable, which way and by how much the receding-horizon rule departs from it. Hand-walked edge cases check policy cost to the cent for a 0/4 task, a 4/4 task, a 1/4 task, and a task with one harness-limit run. Against real data, the extraction is reconciled with what Bai et al. state in numbers, their token-consumption comparison and their GPT-5 price schedule (their per-model results are published only as charts), and with an earlier extraction of the GPT-5 archive.

## 9. Boundaries

The data are clean restarts: each attempt starts fresh from the task, and in a cascade the second configuration starts fresh rather than inheriting a partial patch, context or tool state. Clean restart is an optimistic comparator for retry with carried context, and the gap between them is not measured here.

The verifier is the benchmark's, with the accuracy of a deterministic test suite and no cost. Production verification has both a cost, priced here as a parameter, and an error rate, whose false accepts are priced at one externally sourced value rather than modeled.

One harness, one benchmark, four attempts per task. The transfer test is across configurations within that setting. Cascades here are sequential; the parallel portfolio an operator who cares about latency would run is a different objective. Task boundaries in production agentic work are fuzzier than a benchmark instance.

The task population is public repositories predating the models' training, so memorized fixes appear as cheap consistent successes that no enterprise workload would contain; the direction of the resulting bias on restart value is not known and is not asserted. OpenAI argued in February 2026 that contamination and test-design artifacts limit SWE-bench Verified as a measure of frontier coding capability. That argument bears on capability comparisons across configurations more than on the policy comparisons within a configuration that this paper's primary result concerns, but it bears on both, and the cross-configuration spread reported here should be read with it in view.

A state rule showing no margin over the best schedule cannot be distinguished from an approximation that lost it; the dynamic program is the successor's first task.

## 10. Recorded at freeze

Written here from the audit record, `audit_record.md`, and `configurations.csv`; the plan is frozen when the audit closes.

**Acquisition, 16 to 19 September 2026.** All nine archives in the release were acquired and their SHA-256 values checked against those Hugging Face records for the files. The `_mini` archives hold one completion-log file per instance and none of the three files the extractor reads (`output.jsonl`, `report.json`, `metadata.json`), so they establish nothing and are not used; the full archive is required for every configuration. Those three files are the data layer. Two further sources are read as evidence: each instance's evaluation folder, to classify evaluation errors, and the per-call completion logs, a second record of every call with its own cost. Inference logs and `.bak` files are skipped unread.

**Configurations.** Seven are included, all running the same agent, CodeActAgent: Claude Sonnet 4 (OpenHands 0.40.0, June 2025), Claude Sonnet 4.5 (0.62.0, December 2025), GPT-5 (0.62.0, December 2025 to January 2026), Kimi K2 (1.1.0, January 2026), Gemini 3 Pro Preview (0.62.0, January 2026), GPT-5.2 (0.62.0, January 2026), and Qwen3 Coder 480B (0.48.0 on a self-hosted server; its run name carries the date 7 July 2025). Harness versions differ, a covariate reported with every table. Two archives are excluded. Claude Sonnet 3.7 (0.31.0, April 2025), under exclusion condition 2: its harness replaced any attempt that reached the 100-call cap with a fresh one and kept only the later try, so its recorded attempts are a sample from which long attempts were removed; at least 140 of its 2,000 recorded attempts replaced one or more tries that ran to the cap, and the discarded tries cost $649 against $1,728 recorded. The second Sonnet 4.5 archive, under condition 1: it is a different agent and harness, OpenHands' software-agent SDK, with a critic that allows up to three attempts per task and keeps the one it accepts. The other Sonnet 4.5 archive is therefore the Sonnet 4.5 configuration. The seven hold 13,999 executions; one Sonnet 4 run lacks one task.

**The attempt pool.** How an attempt stopped and whether it resolved are recorded separately, because the harness evaluates whatever diff exists when it stops, including at its own limit or on a refusal. An attempt enters the pool when it is a draw from the configuration's behaviour and its outcome was observed. Excluded: infrastructure failures (outages, rate limits, an upstream error relayed by a router without detail, the harness's eight-hour timeout) and evaluations that returned no verdict. Retained and scored by the evaluation report: finishes; empty patches and patches that did not apply, as failures; stops at the iteration limit or by the loop detector; provider refusals, which are the provider's response to the attempt and which the operator pays for; and errors caused by the model's own output, such as a tool call malformed badly enough to crash the sandbox. The pool holds 13,681 of the 13,999 attempts. Prespecified sensitivities: excluding refusals; counting evaluations without a verdict as unresolved; dropping executions the harness re-ran.

**Stopping rules and the iteration cap.** No configuration has a per-task budget cap. The loop detector is active throughout and fired in Gemini (190 attempts, 29 of which the report resolves), Kimi (4) and GPT-5 (1). GPT-5 stops at 100 iterations and the other six at 500. The protocol assumed 100 throughout and made any other value an exclusion; applied as written, that would have removed six of the seven configurations on a premise the data contradict. The cap is a configuration attribute instead. Each configuration is evaluated over cutoffs up to its own cap, with the no-cutoff baseline at the harness's own cap and flagged in every table. A common-horizon version, every configuration at cutoffs up to 100 calls with any attempt still running at 100 stopped and scored as a failure, is a sensitivity rather than the primary, because between 2 and 21 percent of the resolved attempts of the 500-cap configurations need more than 100 calls, and a common cap would measure a harness setting rather than the configuration. GPT-5 seldom reaches its cap (10 attempts), so the own-cap comparison is close to like for like. Transfer features use absolute call counts, not fractions of the cap.

**Iteration-to-call mapping.** One LLM call per agent step, with no history condensation in any configuration. Where a model issues parallel tool calls, one call yields several actions: GPT-5.2 in 449 executions, Gemini in 51, Sonnet 4 in 15. The decision grid is in calls.

**Source of replicate variation.** The configured temperature is not always the applied one, so the applied value is recorded. GPT-5 and GPT-5.2 sample at their default temperature whatever is configured, GPT-5 because the snapshot accepts only its default and GPT-5.2 because it accepts a temperature only with reasoning off and reasoning was on. Sonnet 4 and Qwen run at 1, Sonnet 4 because extended thinking requires it; Sonnet 4.5, Gemini and Kimi run at 0. The divergence data broadly agree. GPT-5 and GPT-5.2 part at the first agent action (median). Sonnet 4 and Qwen part at the second, with a third or more of tasks parting at the first. At temperature 0, Sonnet 4.5 parts at the second action, with a tenth of tasks parting at the first. Kimi and Gemini part at the third, though a fifth to over a quarter of their tasks part at the first. No task in any configuration has four identical runs. Gemini alone repeats itself, with two or three identical runs on four tasks, which the enumeration prices as the behaviour it is (Section 5): a retry of that configuration can repeat the same trajectory.

**Harness re-runs and the second record of calls.** Every call also leaves a completion log carrying its own cost. For every execution with logs, the logs of the final try are checked against the run's recorded calls and cost. Where the logs outnumber the calls, the harness crashed on an earlier try, re-ran the instance, and kept only the final try; the discarded tries are the earlier logs in time. Every configuration has such executions, from 12 for GPT-5 to 108 for GPT-5.2, and in every included configuration the discarded tries ended before the cap (bar one Sonnet 4 try that reached exactly 500), so they are crashes. The final try is used, and in every configuration it starts fresh rather than continuing the crashed try: its first prompt is 0.99 to 1.03 times the size of every other execution's. For every execution with logs, the final try's logs match the run's recorded calls and cost exactly. The discarded tries have no verdict and stay out of the pool, like other infrastructure failures, and their count and spend are reported per configuration in `configurations.csv`.

**Reasoning tokens.** GPT-5, GPT-5.2 and Gemini report reasoning tokens, billed within output tokens, at 77, 63 and 12 percent of output. Kimi reports the field at zero. The Anthropic configurations do not report thinking separately, but their output count includes it, and Qwen's instruct model does not think. No configuration's cost is therefore a lower bound.

**Price schedules.** Each configuration is priced at its model developer's published list price on the run's first day, over the token classes the developer bills, with any long-context tier. In dollars per million input, cached-input and output tokens:

- GPT-5: $1.25, $0.125 and $10.
- GPT-5.2: $1.75, $0.175 and $14.
- Gemini 3 Pro Preview: $2, $0.20 and $12 up to 200k prompt tokens; $4, $0.40 and $18 above.
- Kimi K2: Moonshot's $0.60, $0.15 and $2.50.
- Both Sonnets: $3 input, $0.30 cache reads, $3.75 five-minute cache writes and $15 output.

Logged cost is not the price. It establishes what the logged prompt count includes, which differs between the two Sonnet configurations, and for GPT-5, Sonnet 4 and Sonnet 4.5 the published schedule reproduces it on every call. For GPT-5.2 and Gemini the harness charged cache reads at the input price, and for Gemini it also ignored the long-context tier. A variant of each schedule with those changes reproduces every call. The price table the harness computed cost with lists the correct prices. The logged cost of these two is 3.4 and 4.3 times what the schedule charges. Kimi's logged cost follows no fixed schedule: Moonshot's price reproduces 41 percent of calls exactly and the total is 19 percent above it, consistent with a router that sends calls to providers at different prices. At the published schedules, the six configurations that log cost spent $12,692 against $23,543 logged.

Qwen was self-hosted and logs neither cost nor call times, so its schedule is imputed and cannot be validated. It is priced at release, 22 July 2025, at the developer's standard price for the model's hosted API, tiered by prompt length, in input, cached-input and output:

- up to 32k tokens: $1, $0.40 and $5;
- to 128k: $1.80, $0.72 and $9;
- to 256k: $3, $1.20 and $15, which no call exceeds.

Qwen's imputed spend is $3,044. The prespecified sensitivity is the lowest third-party price for the same weights, $0.22 input with cache reads at that price and $1.80 output, under which it is $1,027. Qwen's dollar figures are marked as imputed wherever they appear.

Temperature is configured at 0.0 with no seed, but the `gpt-5` snapshot accepts only its default temperature and the client drops the parameter, so the four runs are samples at the model's default temperature, not greedy decodes: a configured setting is not necessarily an applied one, and the audit records the applied value for every configuration. Resolution comes from the evaluation report joined to the execution record on instance id, and the difficulty annotation is carried in the execution record, so *H_i* requires no external join. Terminal states: three executions ended in service unavailability or an eight-hour runtime timeout and are excluded. Ninety-three ended in provider refusals and are retained as a stop scored by the evaluation report, which resolves 18 of them: a refusal is the provider's response to the attempt, the operator pays for the attempt, and the harness scores what it produced. Excluding refusals is the prespecified sensitivity. Eleven executions stopped at the harness's own rules, ten at the iteration limit and one at the loop detector; the report resolves two of them, because the harness evaluates whatever diff exists when it stops. **Found, not anticipated: unequal usable draws.** Excluding attempts leaves some tasks with fewer than four usable draws, most in the configurations with outages: Gemini, GPT-5.2 and Kimi keep four draws on 410, 405 and 412 tasks, and Gemini and GPT-5.2 each have one task with none. A policy of *K* attempts is estimated on tasks with at least *K* usable draws, with the task count reported for every *K*; comparisons across *K* rest on the subset with four usable draws for every configuration involved, with the all-tasks version as a sensitivity. Cascades enumerate all available cross-configuration draw combinations, which requires one draw per configuration and is unbiased at any count.

The primary comparison in Section 7 was set on that magnitude before any policy was evaluated.

**The decision grid and the state rule.** The plan assumed a cap of 100, so its grid stopped at 100 calls. With the cap a configuration attribute, the grid continues every twenty-fifth call to each configuration's own cap, and every policy acts on the same points (Section 6). The state rule's class is fixed in Section 6: three state variables, a logistic and a log-link quasi-Poisson regression pooled across decision points, eight coefficients per configuration. Elapsed time, among the registered transfer features, is dropped from the state because Qwen logs no call times.

**φ** is 0.34 and 0.50. The low end is SWE-Gate's 221 of 644 test-passing patches that failed review-derived constraints. The high end is METR's finding that roughly half of test-passing SWE-bench Verified pull requests would not be merged. Under φ each accepted attempt adds φ·*H_i* to its task's cost, for the outside option paid when a false accept comes back, and policies are re-selected with that term in the objective.
